Lead · Drug deliveryNature Nanotechnology · Sep 8, 2026 · Murthy lab, UC Berkeley

A lipid that is positive in the endosome and negative in the blood

The problem

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) carry mRNA into cells. Their key ingredient is an ionizable lipid: a fat molecule with an amine (nitrogen) head group that picks up a proton and turns positive in acid. That charge does two jobs. It binds mRNA, which is negatively charged, while the particle is mixed in acidic buffer. After a cell swallows the particle into an endosome (a membrane bubble that turns acidic), it helps the mRNA escape into the cell.

The same positive charge also sets off inflammation through TLR4 (an immune receptor that normally detects bacteria) and complement (a cascade of blood proteins that tags invaders). Breaking out of the endosome damages its membrane, which triggers more. So LNPs are limited to low, infrequent doses.

Your turn · 10 seconds

You need the lipid positive at pH 4 to 6 and not positive at pH 7.4. What could you change about the head group?

The idea · two groups that respond to pH in opposite directions

Put a carboxylic acid (–COOH) on the same head group as the amine. In acid, the carboxylic acid keeps its proton and is neutral while the amine is positive, so the lipid is positive. At pH 7.4 the acid loses its proton and becomes negative (–COO⁻), cancelling the amine, so the lipid is neutral to negative. The team calls these switchable lipids. Their best particles became neutral between pH 4.2 and 5.8. That crossover is the pKa, the pH where half the molecules have switched.

Back of the envelope

f = 1 / (1 + 10(pH − pKa))

f = fraction of switching groups still holding their proton; pH = acidity of the surroundings; pKa = the pH where half have switched. Each pH unit past the pKa shifts the ratio 10-fold. For a pKa of 5.0 at blood pH 7.4, f = 1/(1 + 102.4) ≈ 0.004, so about 99.6% of groups have switched. (Idealized; a real particle surface is messier.)

What they did
  1. Built a library. Made 144 switchable lipids, varying three features: the carbon chain between the amine and the acid (5 to 9 carbons), how branched the fatty tails were, and a spacer inside the tails.
  2. Screened in cells. Loaded each with mRNA for luciferase (the firefly glow enzyme; brighter cells mean more mRNA delivered) and dosed HEK cells, a standard human cell line, at 500 ng/mL for 24 h. Benchmark: MC3, the ionizable lipid in an FDA-approved drug.
  3. Measured charge vs. pH. Added a fluorescent dye whose signal depends on surface charge, across pH 2 to 12, to find each particle's pKa.
  4. Tested delivery in mice. Injected the top 18 into the bloodstream and imaged whole-body glow. The best then carried mRNA for EPO (a hormone that is easy to measure in blood) at 0.1 mg/kg, with blood drawn 24 h later.
  5. Stress-tested inflammation. Mice first got LPS (a bacterial molecule that inflames the body, mimicking a sick patient), then particles at 1 mg/kg; blood cytokines (immune alarm proteins) were measured at 4 and 24 h. Human white blood cells from 4 donors were tested for 14 cytokines. Separate assays checked each suspected trigger: complement in human serum, TLR4 reporter cells, and galectin-8 reporter cells (galectin-8 piles onto damaged endosomes).
  6. Treated a disease model. Mice with LPS-injured lungs got mRNA for IL-22, a protein that protects the lung lining, at 2.5 mg/kg into the airway. Readouts 24 h later: protein leaking into the lungs and immune cells flooding in.

The surprise: the particles released mRNA without visibly damaging endosomes. They caused almost no galectin-8 pile-up and did not burst red blood cells at pH 5.5, while every benchmark did. Instead, they collected in Rab11 endosomes, a recycling compartment that other work has shown can let cargo out. In the lung model, protein leakage fell back to healthy levels; with every benchmark LNP, the lungs stayed leaky.

Invention move 02 · again
Let the surroundings flip the switch.

When a design needs opposite properties in different places, tie the property to something that already differs between those places, such as pH, temperature, enzymes, or salt.

For your work

Antibody formulations are often buffered slightly acidic and then land in tissue near pH 7.4. Could a particle surface or excipient be built to change charge across that same jump at injection?

Limits

Mice (3 per group in most experiments) and blood cells from 4 human donors; no people. These particles go mainly to liver and spleen. Whether the new lipids build up inside cells over repeated doses was not measured.
